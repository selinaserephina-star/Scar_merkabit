## 1. One paragraph

SM-058 found one board where the shared grammar W ∩ R^k W R^{−k} exceeds
the centralizer of R^k without any power of R being a Weyl element: the
D₅ half-spin, sixteen states, the even sign patterns on five axes. This
stone locates the anomaly and rules out the obvious explanations. **It
lives on the 4-cube:** the B₄ spinor board — all sixteen sign patterns
on four axes, whose Weyl group is the hyperoctahedral group Aut(Q₄) of
order 384 — has the same poset and the identical rowmotion, W(B₄) sits
inside W(D₅), and the half-turn anomaly (a shared grammar of order 8
against a centralizer of order 4) is already there, with the same four
extra elements: two signed 4-cycles on e₁, e₄, e₂, e₃ and two
involutions. Only the lag-3 survivor, the coordinate permutation
(e₁ e₃)(e₂ e₅), needs the fifth axis, and it is the one survivor that
mixes the clock's two free 8-orbits; all half-turn survivors preserve
them, as does w₀ = −(e₁e₂e₃e₄), which reverses the clock. **It is not
a hidden linearity over F₂** (no power of the D₅, D₆ or D₇ clock is
affine on the even code; D₄'s half-turn is, being linear), **not a
near-miss of linearity** (the altitude of R⁴ is 8 of 16, of R³ 7), and
**not the overgroup mechanism** that explains the only other board with
the same signature: on the B₃ spinor the half-turn is a Weyl element of
the larger W(D₄) ⊃ W(B₃), which makes its shared grammar (16) exceed its
centralizer (8) for a visible reason, whereas on D₅ the half-turn
together with W generates the whole alternating group A₁₆. The
registered guess that the anomaly is specific to the 4-cube held for
B₅ (I_k = C_k for every k) and failed for B₃ in the informative way just
described. The registered guess about the structure of the half-turn's
shared grammar held in its group part — dihedral of order 8, generated
by the centralizer and one signed 4-cycle — and failed in its dynamics:
conjugation by the half-turn permutes the four extras among themselves
without inverting them. The anomaly stands, with four doors closed.

## 3. What was learned

- The anomaly is a fact about the hyperoctahedral group acting on the
  vertices of the 4-cube with the spinor clock. That is a very small
  object: 16 points, 384 symmetries, a clock of order 8 with two free
  orbits. It should be understandable by hand, and it has resisted four
  explanations.
- On every board the survivors of the half-turn preserve the clock's
  orbits, and on D₅ the one survivor that does not is the one at lag 3,
  a pure permutation of axes. Coordinate permutations commuting or
  nearly commuting with the clock recur (D₆'s (e₂ e₄)(e₃ e₆)); whatever
  the anomaly is, it involves how the clock pairs the axes.
- The B₃ board shows what a non-anomaly with the same numbers looks like:
  the half-turn is linear one Weyl group up. D₅ is not that: its half-turn
  is as far from every Weyl group as a permutation can be, generating
  A₁₆ with W. The anomaly is the genuine article.

## 5. Synthesis line

The strangest board in the family is the smallest kind of object there
is — sixteen corners of a four-dimensional cube with a clock that
visits half of them and then the other half — and it keeps a symmetry
across the half-turn that no centralizer, no linearity and no overgroup
accounts for.
